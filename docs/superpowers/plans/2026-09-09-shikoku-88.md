# Shikoku 88 Implementation Plan

> **For agentic workers:** REQUIRED SUB-SKILL: Use superpowers:subagent-driven-development (recommended) or superpowers:executing-plans to implement this plan task-by-task. Steps use checkbox (`- [ ]`) syntax for tracking.

**Goal:** Replace `routes/shikoku-88` — ten stages that do not chain — with four dōjō sections carrying about 45 mechanically-derived day stages, under the corpus's first `legs` pilgrimage and its first `circular` one.

**Architecture:** Four sections (`shikoku-88-awa`, `shikoku-88-tosa`, `shikoku-88-iyo`, `shikoku-88-sanuki`) declaring a `shikoku-88` pilgrimage of `kind: legs` with `circular: true`. Each section's walked line is built from pinned henro-trail relations only; day stages are cut mechanically from that line by a stated rule, never lifted from a guidebook.

**Tech Stack:** TypeScript under `tsx`, `node:test` + `node:assert/strict`, Ajv against `schema/*.json`, no runtime dependencies.

**Spec:** `docs/superpowers/specs/2026-09-05-pilgrimages-and-sections-design.md`, especially §4.2 (chaining and the circular close), §4.3 (relation-less routes), §5.3 (this PR).

## What the measurements already establish

**Measured before this plan was written**, from `.cache/osm/shikoku-88.json` (Overpass, fetched 2026-04-09) and `routes/shikoku-88/waypoints.geojson`. These findings change the shape of the work and one of them contradicts the spec.

**The spec's premise about the OSM data is wrong, in our favour.** §4.3 says "an Overpass name query pulls in every spur and variant; the 4,020 km Shikoku line came from 89 relations", implying the 89 are junk to be filtered. They are not. **They are the trail itself, segmented temple-to-temple**, each named `四国遍路 N番札所X~M番札所Y`, all tagged `route=hiking`:

- **88 of them form the complete sequential circuit** — 1→2, 2→3, … 87→88, and `88番札所大窪寺~1番札所霊山寺`, the closing return to Temple 1. **No link is missing.**
- **They measure 1,145.8 km**, against the declared 1,200 km — a 4.5% gap of exactly the kind a measured line normally shows against a published figure. Not 4,020 km. Whatever produced that number, it was not these relations.
- **One relation is an alternative**: `13669296`, `16番札所観音寺~18番札所恩山寺`, 19.07 km, which **skips Temple 17**. It is not part of the circuit.

**The segments chain cleanly.** Join gaps between consecutive segments: **median 34 m, p90 81 m**. All 88 start within 300 m of their named from-temple.

**Four relations stop short of the temple they are named for**, and are the only real defect:

| segment | ends this far from its to-temple | the next segment starts |
|---|---|---|
| 3→4 | 431 m | — |
| 10→11 | 507 m | 46 m from Temple 11 |
| 19→20 | 2,839 m | 62 m from Temple 20 |
| **22→23** | **9,187 m** | 32 m from Temple 23 |

The successor always picks up at the temple, so these are **incomplete relations upstream, not wrong ones**. `22→23` is the worst and the most consequential: Temple 23 (Yakuō-ji) is the **Awa/Tosa boundary**, where §4.2 requires a stage to end at the boundary temple itself.

**All 88 temples are already waypoints** — `templeNumber` 1–88 complete, typed `sacred_site`, carrying coordinates, `stageIndex` and `kmFromStart`.

**Dōjō lengths** from the chain: Awa (T1→T23) 22 segments, 155.2 km; Tosa (T23→T39) 16, 420.6 km; Iyo (T39→T65) 26, 366.7 km; Sanuki (T65→T88→T1) 24, 203.3 km.

**So the day cut is arithmetically comfortable:** 1,145.8 km at the spec's 25–30 km gives 38–46 days, and at 26 km gives 44. The spec's "about 45" is right, and the sections land near 6 / 16 / 14 / 8.

**The cache is five months old.** Everything above may have improved — the four short-enders especially. Task 1 re-fetches before anything is pinned.

## Decisions already made

- **Exclude the 16→18 alternative.** The pilgrimage visits all 88 temples in order; a variant skipping Temple 17 is not the route. Record it in `osm.note` so the next reader does not rediscover it as a missing relation.
- **Boundary relations belong to the section that walks them.** Awa owns 1→2 … 22→23 and **ends at Temple 23**. Tosa owns 23→24 … 38→39 and **begins at Temple 23**. Iyo owns 39→40 … 64→65. Sanuki owns 65→66 … 87→88 **and 88→1**, the closing return. This is what §4.2's "a boundary stage ends and starts at the boundary place itself" requires.
- **`overview.topology` becomes `linear` on every section.** Each dōjō is individually linear — Awa runs Temple 1 to 23 and does not return. The circuit is claimed by the pilgrimage block's `circular: true`, which every section repeats. §4.2 is explicit that no honest section-level topology could arm the circuit check.
- **`shikoku-88` stops being a route id and becomes a pilgrimage id**, exactly as `kumano-kodo` did in 1.8.0. This is the release's one consumer-breaking change.

```bash

```

Then re-run every measurement in "What the measurements already establish" above and record which still hold. Report each figure that moved.

- [ ] **Step 2: Rule on the four short-ending relations**

For each of 3→4, 10→11, 19→20 and 22→23, establish whether the shortfall survives the refetch, and if so **why** — an unmapped final approach, a relation that genuinely ends at a trailhead, or a temple whose node sits off the trail it is reached from.

The options, in order of preference:

1. **It is fixed upstream.** Nothing to do.
2. **The successor relation covers the missing stretch.** Then the walked line is continuous even though one relation is short, and the anchor still resolves. Prove it by measuring, not by assuming.
3. **The gap is real and unbridged.** Then §4.3 applies: `refuseIncompleteLine` must not be spliced around. Say plainly which section this sinks and what it would take to fix, and stop — **do not invent geometry**.

`22→23` decides whether Awa can ship at all, because Temple 23 is its terminus and §4.2 forbids a boundary stage ending anywhere else.

- [ ] **Step 3: Write the research note**

Record in `.superpowers/sdd/shikoku-osm-research.md`: the pinned relation id list per section, the measured length per section, the ruling on each short relation, and the excluded 16→18 alternative with its id and why. Later tasks read this file rather than re-deriving it.

If any section cannot be built, say so now. A section that cannot be cut ships `metadataOnly` per §4.3 and waits for a later release — that is a legitimate outcome, and it is far cheaper to discover here than in Task 4.

## Task 4: About forty-five days, cut mechanically

The heart of the PR, and the part that must be **reproducible rather than authored**.

**Files:** `routes/shikoku-88-*/stages.json`

- [ ] **Step 1: Implement the rule exactly as §5.3 states it**

> From the previous day's temple, the day runs to the next temple in sequence that puts it between 25 and 30 km along the walked line, or at that temple's town where the temple has no lodging.

Two clarifications the spec implies and you must make explicit in the derivation note:

- **What "no lodging" means, measurably.** Decide it from `waypoints.geojson` (an `accommodation` waypoint within some radius of the temple) and state the radius. A rule you cannot re-run is not mechanical.
- **What happens when no temple falls in 25–30 km.** Long stretches — Tosa's 420 km over 16 temple-gaps averages 26 km but individual gaps run much longer. State the tie-break (nearest to 27.5 km? first over 25?) and apply it uniformly.

- [ ] **Step 2: Boundary stages end at the boundary temple, never its town**

§4.2 and §5.3 both say this. Awa's last stage ends at Temple 23; Tosa's first begins there. The lodging fallback **does not apply** at a boundary. Sanuki's final stage runs Ōkubo-ji (88) → Ryōzen-ji (1).

- [ ] **Step 3: Record the derivation in `provenance.sources`**

Each section carries an entry with `dataTypes: ["stages", "distances"]` naming the rule, the line it was measured on, and the date. This is what makes the cut citable instead of lifted.

- [ ] **Step 4: Verify the count and the spread**

Report the stage count per section and overall, and the distance distribution. If the total is far from ~45, the rule was misapplied — say so rather than adjusting the rule to hit the number.

- [ ] **Step 5: Commit**

## Task 7: The review, by agents who did not write it

**This task must go to agents with no part in Task 6.** That separation is the whole of its value.

~45 stages is more than one reader should judge in one pass. Split by section, one reader per dōjō, each judging only its own — and none of them the drafter.

- [ ] **Step 1: Judge each field against the data, not against plausibility**

Check every claim: do the endpoint ids resolve and are they the places named; does the measured distance match `ways/report.json`; does every place exist in `waypoints.geojson`.

- [ ] **Step 2: Strike what cannot be traced**

A shorter, duller line that is true beats an evocative one that is not.

- [ ] **Step 3: Clear and tick, in one commit per section**

Remove `"drafted": true` only from stages whose text stands, and tick their lines in the same commit — CI fails a flag that comes off without its tick.

**If a reader rewrites a stage's text, the flag stays on and it says so.** Prose you authored cannot be cleared by you; it needs another reader. That is the gate working, not a failure.

- [ ] **Step 4: Remember the text lives in three places**

`stages.json` is the source; `ways/stage-NN.json` embeds it and is regenerated by `build-ways`; the section's `docs/*.html` inlines it **by hand** and no generator will fix it. Editing text means touching all three.

## What this plan does not do

- **It does not invent geometry.** If a relation gap cannot be closed, the section ships `metadataOnly` and waits (§4.3). Task 1 decides this before any work depends on it.
- **It does not lift a guidebook's stage pacing.** The day cut is mechanical and its rule is recorded, because a pacing this repo could neither cite nor relicense is not shippable.
- **It does not add the 16→18 alternative.** The route visits all 88 temples in order.
- **It does not carry Shikoku's legends into stage text.** §6 admits only what the section's own data holds, and this is the route where that restraint costs the most and matters the most.
