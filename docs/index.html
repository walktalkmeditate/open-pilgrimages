Why This Exists

There is no comprehensive, structured, open dataset of pilgrimage routes. Trail databases capture geometry and elevation but miss what makes a pilgrimage a pilgrimage — the credential systems, sacred sites, cultural practices, seasonal ceremonies, and the interior transformation that unfolds over weeks of walking.

Open Pilgrimages captures three layers of data:

LayerWhat It Contains
GeometryRoute paths and waypoints as standard GeoJSON
LogisticsStages, accommodation, water sources, services, seasonal availability
Cultural & SpiritualTraditions, credential systems, associated literature, sacred site protocols, interior journey narratives

Routes

Camino de Santiago (Frances) Christian

764 km Linear France → Spain 33 stages

The most popular pilgrimage route to Santiago de Compostela, crossing the Pyrenees from France and traversing northern Spain. Walked continuously since the 9th century.

Fully enriched — geometry, waypoints, stages, interior journey

Camino del Norte (Northern Way) Christian

788 km Linear Spain 34 stages

The coastal Camino de Santiago along the Bay of Biscay, from Irún through the Basque Country, Cantabria, and Asturias into Galicia. The longest non-Francés Camino, and the only one that stays within sight of the sea for most of its length.

Fully enriched — geometry, waypoints, stages, interior journey

Camino Primitivo (Original Way) Christian

263 km Linear Spain 11 stages

The original Camino, walked by King Alfonso II of Asturias from Oviedo to Santiago in 814 CE. The most physically demanding of the major Caminos, crossing the Asturian and Galician mountains via the high Puerto del Palo pass.

Fully enriched — geometry, waypoints, stages, interior journey

Camino Portugués (Central) Christian

243 km Linear Portugal → Spain 11 stages

The second most-walked route to Santiago de Compostela, traversing the Minho region of northern Portugal before crossing into Galicia at Tui. Walked since the 12th century, shorter and flatter than the Camino Francés.

Fully enriched — geometry, waypoints, stages, interior journey

Camino Inglés (English Way) Christian

112 km Linear Spain 6 stages

The shortest of the major Caminos, walked since medieval times by English, Irish, Scandinavian, and Flemish pilgrims arriving by sea at Ferrol and A Coruña. Modern revival began in the 2000s.

Fully enriched — geometry, waypoints, stages, interior journey

Kumano Kodo Shinto / Buddhist

36 km (Nakahechi) Network Japan 4 stages

Ancient pilgrimage routes through the mountains of the Kii Peninsula to the three Grand Shrines of Kumano. UNESCO World Heritage since 2004, and sister route to the Camino de Santiago through the Dual Pilgrim program.

Fully enriched — geometry, waypoints, stages, interior journey
Two of the four routes — the Iseji and the Ōhechi — are sections of their own that ship metadata only, because OpenStreetMap does not yet hold enough of either trail to build a walked line. Help complete them

Kohechi (Mountain Route) Shinto / Buddhist

63 km Linear Japan 4 stages

The mountain route of the Kumano Kodo: Kōyasan south to Kumano Hongu Taisha over three passes above 1,000 m, walked in four days between the river-valley settlements of Omata, Miura-guchi and Totsukawa Onsen.

Geometry, four stages and elevation measured on the walked line, all four stages' text reviewed and cleared, and 35 waypoints — those measured against this section's own relation instead
The waypoints stop 1.8 km short of Kumano Hongu Taisha — the stretch of the walked line that falls outside the corridor searched against that relation. Help complete it

Awa (Temples 1-23) Buddhist

154.5 km Linear Japan 5 stages

The first of the four dōjō of the Shikoku 88: 23 of the eighty-eight temples in 154.5 km, from Temple 1 to Temple 23, where Tosa takes over from the same point.

Fully enriched — geometry, waypoints, 5 day stages cut from the walked line, and interior journey text reviewed and cleared

Tosa (Temples 23-39) Buddhist

418.5 km Linear Japan 15 stages

The longest and emptiest dōjō: 418.5 km for sixteen temples, and the circuit's widest gap between two of them — 82.7 km from Temple 37 to Temple 38.

Fully enriched — geometry, waypoints, 15 day stages cut from the walked line, and interior journey text reviewed and cleared

Iyo (Temples 39-65) Buddhist

365.7 km Linear Japan 14 stages

The dōjō with the most temples, twenty-six in 365.7 km, and every spacing the circuit has: a 70.4 km step between two of them, and a run of five each under 5 km.

Fully enriched — geometry, waypoints, 14 day stages cut from the walked line, and interior journey text reviewed and cleared
Two of the fourteen days carry nothing between their own start and end — the section clears the coverage bar on the other twelve. Help fill them in

Sanuki (Temples 65-88, and the return to Temple 1) Buddhist

202.2 km Linear Japan 6 stages

The dōjō that closes the circle: Temple 65 to Temple 88, where the numbering ends, then the 39.8 km return to Temple 1 where the pilgrimage began.

Fully enriched — geometry, waypoints, 6 day stages cut from the walked line, and interior journey text reviewed and cleared
