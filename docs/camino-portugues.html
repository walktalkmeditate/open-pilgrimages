Camino Portugués (Central)

Porto to Santiago · the second most-walked Camino

The second most-walked route to Santiago de Compostela, traversing the Minho region of northern Portugal before crossing the Miño river border at Tui and continuing through Galicia. Walked since the 12th century. Shorter, flatter, and less crowded than the Camino Francés, with a distinctive sequence of Romanesque churches, medieval stone villages, and the iconic river-crossing from Portugal into Spain.

Tradition & Credential

Saint: Saint James the Great

Medieval pilgrimage route from Portugal to the shrine of the apostle Saint James. Documented pilgrim traffic grew from the 12th century onward, following Portuguese independence in 1139 and reusing sections of the Roman Via XIX, which passed through Ponte de Lima, Tui, and Caldas de Reis. Queen Isabel of Portugal (1271–1336), later canonized as Saint Isabel, walked to Santiago in 1325 after the death of her husband King Dinis. The Portuguese Way became one of the most important routes to Santiago after the Camino Francés, and today is the second most-walked route by a wide margin.

UNESCO status: Not independently UNESCO-inscribed.

Credential: Pilgrim Credential (Credencial del Peregrino / Credencial do Peregrino), stamped — sellos in Spain, carimbos in Portugal — at least twice daily. From the Portuguese border at Tui it is 115 km to Santiago, just enough to qualify for the Compostela, making Tui the most common starting point for pilgrims walking the minimum distance. Credentials are issued at Porto Cathedral, the Cathedral Pilgrim Office in Tui, the Confraria de Santiago in Porto, and the Confraternity of Saint James (UK).

Variants

Three variants extend the Camino Portugués. The Coastal route ships as a full route with its own geometry, stages, and statistics; Espiritual and Lisboa are metadata-only stubs.

Coastal (Camino Portugués da Costa — Portuguese Section)

The Portuguese coastal section of the Camino Portugués da Costa, running from Porto along the Atlantic coast through Vila do Conde, Esposende, Viana do Castelo, and Caminha to the border ferry crossing at A Guarda. Approximately 110 km in 5 stages. The Spanish continuation through Oia, Baiona, Vigo, and Redondela (where it merges with the Central route to Santiago) is currently a stub; full Spanish-side data is planned for a future release. The Coastal Way is the second-most-walked Camino Portugués route after the Central, accounting for ~17% of all 2025 Compostelas — nearly tied with Central. It grew from very small numbers in the 2010s to 89,511 pilgrims in 2025.

The Coastal Way (Caminho da Costa) is the historically older Atlantic-shore variant, walked since at least the 12th century by pilgrims who preferred the seaward route — documented users include Thomas Becket, Archbishop of Canterbury. It was officially recognized as a Camino by the Cathedral of Santiago in 2016 and fully marked by 2017. It shares the Camino Portugués credential system described above.
